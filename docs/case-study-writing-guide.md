# Writing a useful portfolio case study

A case study should let a reader understand the work, your contribution, and a consequential decision before asking them to read the full process. Keep each project’s own question and artifacts at the centre.

## What to write

1. **Opening:** name the project and explain the situation it addresses in one or two sentences. Show an actual artifact or working result. Link the prototype if one exists.
2. **Contribution and status:** state what you did, what others did, the scope, and whether the work is a concept, prototype, analysis, or evaluated product. Use dates only when verified.
3. **Central question:** describe the tension that makes this project interesting. Avoid a generic description of a design process.
4. **Two or three consequential choices:** connect an observation or constraint to a decision, the resulting behaviour, and a tradeoff. Use real alternatives or iterations only when documented.
5. **Outcome:** say what was actually produced or demonstrated. Distinguish the artifact, participant feedback, and measured change; they are different kinds of result.
6. **Next question:** identify what remains uncertain and how you would investigate it. A specific next evaluation is more useful than a broad claim of success.
7. **Credits and methods:** attribute collaborators, tools, datasets, references, and assets. Put detailed method notes after the main story or in an optional disclosure.

## What a visual needs to do

Every visual should help someone inspect the artifact, understand a decision, compare genuine alternatives, or assess evidence. Caption what it shows, where it came from, and any essential interpretation limit. Label explanatory diagrams as schematics. Never present a new illustrative drawing as an original research artifact or a tested screen.

Original screenshots, photographed prototypes, state sequences, annotated maps, and charts with denominators are useful. Decorative mockups cannot replace missing work. Give charts enough room to read and offer the full-size source.

## Before adding stronger claims

For each claim, keep a source or author confirmation. In particular, do not invent interview locations/counts, participant quotations, measured task improvements, original alternatives, or product efficacy. The author’s account is still needed to establish undocumented intent and iterations. Add those stories when confirmed rather than filling the gap with a plausible design narrative.

A useful drafting pattern is: “I observed [specific evidence]. I chose [documented change] because [reason]. This meant [tradeoff]. The prototype/test showed [actual result]. I still need to evaluate [specific uncertainty].” Omit any part whose evidence is absent; do not complete the formula by invention.
